Running Fitness App

Overview

Role: Lead — Team of 6

I led a team of 6 through the design of a running fitness app aimed at making the sport approachable for beginners. As lead, I assigned tasks, delegated ownership of individual screens and features, and set weekly deadlines to keep the team on track toward our final prototype across a 10-week product cycle.

Opening Fit_app — the first interaction, from opening the app to setting goals and landing on the main interface.

Problem

Across the existing running apps we researched, we found a consistent issue: new runners were met with an overwhelming amount of information the moment they opened the app. Metrics, permissions, and settings were presented all at once, with little distinction between what a beginner actually needed to see and what was meant for a more experienced runner. For someone just starting the habit of running, this made the experience feel intimidating rather than motivating. We wanted to design an app where a first-time user could immediately understand what they were being asked to do, and what their post-run data actually meant, without being buried in options.

Research

We began by identifying the core goals a new runner wants to set: general running habits, step goals, distance goals, and outdoor activity goals. From there, we explored features to support those goals, including a live elevation bar that updates as the user runs, an activity ring that tracks progress toward their overall goal, a friends-only social layer where users can see each other's progress, and an AI assistant available for questions or advice during a run.

We validated these ideas through user testing, and the clearest finding was about the interface itself, not the features: too many buttons on the main screen caused confusion. One tester summed it up directly: "There was a lot of tapping to get to the page." In response, we restructured the UI around just five core buttons, with every screen offering a way back to the main page — so a user is never more than one tap from a reset point, no matter how deep into the app they are.

Solution

One problem we kept hearing about was runners wanting to know exactly what their pace or location looked like at a specific point in their run — for example, "what was my pace at mile two?" Our solution was an interactive route map: users can tap directly on a point along their run path (like a mile marker) and see the exact pace, time, and location data for that moment, rather than only getting averages for the whole run.

Discoveries

The hardest part of this process was minimizing the number of screens. Every detailed interaction we added tended to spawn a new screen, and the more of those we created, the more time-consuming and heavy the app felt to navigate — the opposite of what we wanted. This mattered more than it might for other apps: because this is a tool meant to be used during a run, we had to be especially disciplined about keeping interaction during the run itself as minimal as possible, saving the more detailed screens for before or after.
